# Narrative Analysis: Racing Game Stories

**Stories turn out to be the most patterned thing in the corpus.** Nearly every
racing game with a plot tells the same one, with the same inciting incident and
the same supporting character.

Two findings matter most: **your premise is the rare structure**, and **no racing
game has ever had a maker protagonist.**

The decisions that follow are specified in `30-NARRATIVE-DESIGN.md`.

---

# PART 1 — The stories

## 1.1 The reclamation cluster (the dominant form)

### NFS: Most Wanted (2005) — the template

18 million copies, the best-selling game in the series.

You arrive in Rockport a newcomer in a BMW M3 GTR. You meet Mia. You race Razor,
Blacklist #15 — **and lose, because he sabotaged your engine at the starting
line.** You lose the race, the car and your freedom. Sgt. Cross arrests you, then
has to release you: no car, no evidence.

Mia collects you, hands you cash, and you pick one of three starter cars —
handling, top speed, or acceleration. Razor has used *your* car to reach #1. You
work up all fifteen, beat him, take the car back.

Then the turn: **Mia is an undercover officer.** She arrests all fifteen, tosses
you the keys, tells you to run. Cross puts you on the National Most Wanted list.

TV Tropes names the structure precisely — **"Start X to Stop X"**: to bring down
the Blacklist she had to create a bigger criminal than any of them.

Structural note: **each Blacklist member is strong in specific event types** —
circuit, sprint, speedtrap, drag, pursuit. The roster is a difficulty curve with
faces on it. Defeating one grants a choice of two reward markers from a set
including pink slips and customisation items.

### The rest of the cluster
- **NFS: Underground 2 (2004)** — reigning champion, ambushed and wrecked by an
  unknown driver. Rachel helps you rebuild in Bayview. Caleb and the Wraiths.
- **NFS: Carbon (2006)** — direct sequel. Return to Palmont, remember why you
  left. Crews, territory, Darius. Cross returns having quit the force to become a
  bounty hunter specifically to catch you.
- **Mad Max (2015)** — Scrotus takes the Interceptor. Chumbucket helps you build
  the Magnum Opus. **The plot is the car.**

## 1.2 The infiltration cluster

**Driver (1999)** — John Tanner, NYPD detective and former racing driver, sent
undercover as a getaway driver. Miami, San Francisco, Los Angeles, New York.
**The garage audition is his job interview with the mob** — the tutorial is a
scene (`21` Part 3).

**Driver: San Francisco (2011)** — Tanner in a coma after Jericho escapes. The
entire game is his fantasy, which is how he can leave his body and possess any
car in the city. **The most formally daring narrative in the genre by a wide
margin**, and the narrative device *is* the mechanic.

## 1.3 The ascent cluster (rare)

**Al Unser Jr.'s Road to the Top (1994)** — karts → snowmobiles → IROC → Indy →
the man himself at Vancouver. His disembodied head appears between races to give
advice. **No villain, no betrayal.**

**NFS ProStreet (2007)** — Ryan Cooper rises through sanctioned racing to face
Ryo Watanabe, the Showdown King.

## 1.4 The absent cluster

**Gran Turismo** — famously no story. GT7's Café added Luca, a curator who tells
you car history as you collect. **The narrative is automotive culture, not plot.**

**Porsche Unleashed** — Evolution mode is chronological history; Factory Driver is
employment. Neither is a story.

**NFS Shift (2009)** — a contemporary review: *you are a nameless driver... there
are no characters or personalities, other drivers are nothing more than names on
the scoreboard.*

**Top Gear, Street Racer, Outlander** — premise only. Top Gear 3000 has a
sci-fi framing (become The Planet's Champ) and no plot.

## 1.5 The player-generated cluster

**Motorsport Manager** — no authored story, but Playsport built a fictional
universe: Panther Precision Racing, Vexala Motorsport, drivers who are
recognisable expies. **Fans built "Real World" mods to replace it all, and
veteran players prefer the fiction** (`16` Part 2).

**Car Parking Multiplayer** — zero story. Players built roleplay servers in the
Philippines, Russia, Jamaica, Mexico — SWAT convoys, presidential escorts, fake
gas stations (`28` §5.2).

# PART 2 — The seven similarities

## 2.1 Reclamation dominates, and it is a design solution

Most Wanted, Underground 2, Carbon, Mad Max. **You had something. It was taken.
You get it back.**

It solves two problems at once: it **justifies starting with nothing** — which
every progression system requires — and it **gives one specific car emotional
weight** before the player has earned any.

## 2.2 Sabotage is the standard inciting incident

Razor tampers with your engine at the line. Underground 2 opens with an ambush.

**It is chosen because it protects the player's dignity.** You did not lose
because you are bad; you lost because you were cheated. And it produces a named
enemy within five minutes.

## 2.3 The mentor is a tutorial wearing a person

Samantha. Rachel. Mia. Rog. Chumbucket. Al Unser Jr.'s floating head. Luca.

**Every one exists to explain systems.** Where to go, what the ladder is, what
the car needs. **The cheapest narrative device in the genre and the most
reliable.**

## 2.4 The ladder of named rivals

The Blacklist's fifteen. Eddie. Caleb. Darius. Ryo Watanabe. Al Unser Jr.

## 2.5 The car is a character

The Magnum Opus. The BMW M3 GTR. Eleanor. The Interceptor. **The car has a name
and the plot is about it.**

## 2.6 The inverse law

**The deeper the simulation, the thinner the story.**

| Deep simulation, no story | Arcade, full story |
|---|---|
| Gran Turismo | Underground 1 & 2 |
| Porsche Unleashed | Most Wanted |
| Shift | Carbon |
| Assetto Corsa, iRacing | Mad Max |

## 2.7 Where authored story is absent, players build it

Motorsport Manager's community defends its fictional lore against real-world
mods. CPM's players built an entire roleplay ecosystem in a game about parking.

**The appetite exists whether or not you supply it.**

# PART 3 — The differences

**Tanner is the only professional.** Every other protagonist is an outsider
breaking in. He has a job and a badge.

**Driver: San Francisco is the only formal experiment.** The coma is not set
dressing — it is the mechanical justification for Shift.

**Al Unser Jr. is the only game where progression *is* the story.** Four
disciplines, each teaching what the next demands (`18` §5.2).

**Mad Max is the only one where the car is the goal** rather than the means.

**GT7 is the only one whose story is education.** Luca teaches you why cars
matter. The only "narrative" in the corpus that would work as a book.

# PART 4 — What follows for your game

## 4.1 You are writing the rare structure

**Your premise is an ascent story, not a reclamation story.** Mechanic → weekend
racer → professional driver.

The only precedents across fifty-plus games are **Al Unser Jr. and ProStreet** —
and **ProStreet's ascent was commercially punished** because the street audience
did not follow it to a racetrack (`19` §2.8).

**Mitigation:** Heat's structure — let both tiers coexist rather than replacing
one with the other (`19` §2.12).

## 4.2 And the genuinely unclaimed one

> **No racing game has a maker protagonist.**
>
> Tanner drives. The Most Wanted protagonist drives. Max drives — **Chumbucket
> builds.** The one character in the entire corpus who does what your
> protagonist does **is a sidekick.**

Your character is defined by *making*, not driving. That is unclaimed territory
in a genre running since 1982.

## 4.3 The finding to sit with

**Nobody has combined deep simulation with an authored story.** The inverse law
(§2.6) holds across the whole corpus.

That is either the gap you are walking into, or the reason it is empty.

**Ford v Ferrari suggests the material works** — an engineering procedural with
genuine narrative weight, told in workshops and at test sessions.

The analysis of *why* the law holds, and the structural answer to it, is in
`30-NARRATIVE-DESIGN.md` Part 5.
