# Case Study: Car Parking Multiplayer

**The most instructive single game in this research package.**

A small Kazakh studio built a social space for car people, wore a parking game as
a hat, and reached 100 million installs with parking-game physics. **Part 5 —
the go-to-market — is the half nobody documents, and it is the half that matters
most to a small team.**

---

# PART 1 — The anatomy of "social space"

CPM works in three layers. **Only the first was built by Olzhass.**

## 1.1 Free walking is the load-bearing mechanic

Everything social depends on it. The moment the player can step out of the car,
three things become possible that exist in no other racing game:

1. **You can stand next to your car** while someone looks at it
2. **You can gather** — a car meet needs bodies, not just vehicles
3. **You can be a character**, which is what makes roleplay possible at all

## 1.2 The supporting systems, and their social function

| System | What it actually enables |
|---|---|
| **Live voice chat** | Real-time build advice. Deal negotiation. Presence. |
| **Real interiors on every car** | You can sit in someone else's build |
| **Gas stations, car services** | **Places to meet.** A world needs landmarks or nobody can say "meet me at—" |
| **Enterable buildings** | Private space. RP scenarios need interiors |
| **Drone mode** | **A camera.** The most underrated feature — it is the export pipeline |
| **Police mode** | Structured conflict without a scoreboard |
| **Taxi / cargo / delivery** | **Jobs.** Reasons to exist in the world that are not racing |
| **Clothing, skins, emotes** | Identity separate from the car |

## 1.3 What is conspicuously absent

**There is no win condition.** No scoreboard, no ranking, no ending.

> **CPM is a *place*, and places do not have win conditions.**

# PART 2 — Why the tuning is that deep

Engine swap, turbo, gearbox, exhaust. Adjustable suspension and **wheel angle** —
camber, in a parking game. Dynamic vinyls, body parts, liveries. And CPM2 adds a
**dyno run**, a **mechanic transmission with clutch**, and a **fuel system**.

The obvious question is why a parking game needs a clutch model. The answer
reframes everything:

> ## The tuning depth exists *because of* the social layer, not despite it
>
> If nobody sees your car, camber adjustment is a number.
>
> If two hundred people are standing at a virtual gas station, **camber is a
> statement.** Stance culture is real car culture, and CPM shipped the tools for
> it.

Every one of those systems is **a vocabulary for self-expression through a car**.
The dyno is not there for lap times — there are no lap times. It is there so you
can say what your build makes.

**This is the argument for your tuning depth in its strongest form.** Depth is
not a simulation feature. It is an expression feature — and expression needs an
audience, even an imagined one.

# PART 3 — The World Sale economy

## 3.1 How it works

Build a car — vinyl wrap, tuned engine, body kit, paint. List it. Other players
browse and buy with in-game money.

**No real-money trading. No item shop. Player-set prices, player to player.**

## 3.2 The three archetypes it created

**The collector.** Buys finished builds. Never modifies anything. Wants the car,
not the work.

**The flipper.** Buys cheap, unloved cars, upgrades them, resells at profit.
Arbitrage on other people's taste and impatience.

**The builder-for-hire.** *Nobody predicted this one.* TikTok is full of posts
like **"Customer wanted 3 cars so you know I had to get him right"** — showing a
Demon Challenger, a purple flame Demon, and a Trackhawk, built to order.

> **A service economy emerged inside a parking game.** People build cars for
> other people, for in-game money, arranged over Discord and delivered in-game.
>
> **That is your protagonist's job.** It emerged spontaneously in a game with
> parking-game physics, because the tools were there and the audience was there.

## 3.3 Why in-game currency works better than real money here

Real-money trading would have destroyed it. In-game currency means **the price of
a car reflects the labour that went into it**, not the wallet of whoever bought
it.

A well-built car has genuine market value **because building it took genuine
time.** That is a functioning labour market.

# PART 4 — The engine-bay show

The vehicle control menu opens doors, trunk and bonnet, and switches the engine
on and off. And there is **a dedicated animation for displaying a tuned engine,
or performing virtual repairs, in front of other players.**

Sit with the fact that somebody built that.

**A parking game contains an animation whose only purpose is to be watched by
another person while you show them your engine.** It has no gameplay effect. It
does not make the car faster. It exists purely so that ownership can be
*performed*.

## What it tells you

> **Building a car is only half the loop. The other half is showing it.**
>
> Every racing game builds the first half. **CPM is the only one in fifty that
> built the second**, and it is sitting on 100 million installs.

`25-GARAGE-DESIGN` already specifies openable panels and an installation
sequence. **CPM proves what they are for** — and that even without multiplayer
the impulse is real. **The substitute for an audience is a camera.**

# PART 5 — The go-to-market

**The undocumented half.**

## 5.1 What Olzhass actually did — three things, no marketing spend

**1. Developed through Discord and community forums**, taking player input
directly and collaborating on events like holiday car meets. **The roadmap is
community-negotiated.**

**2. Anchored in one regional car culture first.** The Turkish player base is
structurally integral — localised servers, Istanbul-inspired areas, culturally
specific events such as Bayram drifts, and Turkish creators (Enes Batur among
them) carrying it through tutorials.

**3. Shipped systems that generate shareable moments, then got out of the way.**

## 5.2 What the players then built — all of it unpaid

**Roleplay servers, worldwide and localised.** Philippines (PNP SWAT convoys,
presidential escort convoys, government scenarios), Russia, Jamaica, Mexico,
Spain. Recruitment runs through Telegram and Discord.

**Clans and crews.** "Royal Stance Crew" running car meetings and giveaways.
"Law Clan Street Racers."

**Scheduled events, livestreamed.** "SRT Meet Part 3." "Drag Meet 5 de Mayo" in
the Mexican community. Livestreamed car meets announced by date.

**Discord marketplaces.** Servers whose entire purpose is buying and selling cars.

**"Build of the Week" shoutouts.** Community-run recognition programmes.

**A pit-crew culture.** From a community write-up: *stuck on a build? Hop into
voice and get real-time help. It's like having a pit crew online.*

**Content genres that did not previously exist.** "No crash challenge." "Park
with no brakes." Fake gas station RP. Cop chases gone wrong.

**And the ultimate marker of cultural penetration: players have rebuilt CPM
inside Roblox.** There are CPM roleplay servers running in another game entirely.

## 5.3 The distribution engine

| Platform | Function |
|---|---|
| **TikTok** | **Top of funnel.** Short clips of drifts and impossible parking jobs. Per one analysis, *these clips blow up overnight and bring in new players daily* |
| **YouTube** | **Retention.** Gaster does roleplay scenarios; Elperry mixes gameplay with mod tips. Long-form, personality-driven, and it teaches the game |
| **Discord** | **Community.** Where the deals, the clans and the events actually live |

Even the mod-APK ecosystem functions as discovery — "unlimited coins glitch" is a
huge search category, and every piece of it is someone talking about the game.

## 5.4 The actual lesson

> **Olzhass did not make content. They made systems that make content.**
>
> A drone camera, free walking, voice chat, an open world with landmarks, and a
> car you can modify beyond recognition — those five things generate an infinite
> supply of TikTok clips, and every clip is an install.
>
> **The players are the marketing department. The systems are the payroll.**

# PART 6 — What you take

**1. Ship the camera.** Drone mode is CPM's export pipeline and nobody talks
about it. Your equivalent is a proper photo mode from the Hero and Engine Bay
stations (`25` §7b) — framed, lit, shareable, with the build spec optionally
burned into the image. **This is an acquisition channel and it costs a camera
rig.**

**2. Make the build shareable as an object, not just an image.** A build code or
spec card another player can read, admire, or replicate. **CPM's economy runs on
cars being legible to other people.**

**3. Design for the performance of ownership, not just ownership.** The bonnet
opens *for someone*. Even solo, that means a garage that photographs well, named
builds (`05` §1.3), and a spec sheet worth screenshotting.

**4. Restoration and resale, single-player.** Buy rough, fix, sell for what the
work is worth. CPM proves the desire without needing its multiplayer, and it is
literally your premise.

**5. Pick one community and build in the open with them.** A marque, a
discipline, or a country. Let their creators carry you. For a small team this is
the only distribution model that has ever worked.

**6. TikTok-shaped moments are a design requirement.** Ask of every system:
**what fifteen-second clip does this produce?**

| System | The clip |
|---|---|
| Installation sequence (`25` Part 6) | ✅ A part going on, tools, the torque-wrench click |
| Instability meter (`20` §1) | ✅ A save at the edge, meter nearly full |
| Tier escalation (`25` Part 7) | ✅ Same job, house tier vs pro shop tier, side by side |
| Damage and repair | ✅ Before and after |
| A tuning menu | ❌ Nothing |

**7. And the thing CPM structurally cannot do.** Its physics can never support a
*driving* clip that is impressive for the driving itself.

> **A perfectly caught slide is a better TikTok than a parking job** — and it is
> the one piece of content they cannot make.
